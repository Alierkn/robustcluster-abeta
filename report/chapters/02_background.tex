\chapter{Scientific and theoretical background}
\label{ch:background}
\section{Amyloid-β40 and intrinsically disordered proteins}
The object being clustered is a set of peptide conformations, not a set of different proteins. Aβ40 belongs to the intrinsically disordered systems for which one structure cannot adequately describe the ensemble \autocite{vanderlee2014,cadenelli2026}. An ensemble includes both the possible conformations and their statistical weights. This distinction matters when comparing a compact configuration with an extended one: observing both does not tell us how probable either is at equilibrium.

Experiments also often report averages over conformations. Combining simulation with NMR or SAXS constraints can help determine an ensemble, provided that the calculated and measured observables are compatible \autocite{borthakur2025}.

\section{Molecular dynamics and conformational ensembles}
Molecular dynamics follows atomic positions by numerically integrating equations of motion. A force field supplies the potential energy and hence the forces; the solvent model and thermodynamic conditions also affect the result. The primary study used GROMACS, the CHARMM36m force field and PLUMED \autocite{cadenelli2026,abraham2015,huang2017,tribello2014}. Here, the supplied descriptors are the starting point for analysis.

At canonical equilibrium, a configuration with potential energy $U(q)$ contributes according to the Boltzmann factor $\exp[-\beta_T U(q)]$. Coordinates are denoted by $q$. The inverse temperature factor is $\beta_T=1/(k_B T)$ for energy per molecule and $1/(RT)$ for molar energy. The energy convention must therefore be known before weights can be calculated.

\section{Sampling limitations}
A trajectory with many saved frames may contain far fewer independent observations. If successive conformations change slowly, saving them more frequently mainly adds similar rows. Correlation analysis estimates this dependence under stated assumptions; it cannot recover structures that were never visited. Equilibration and sampling quality consequently need more evidence than trajectory length alone \autocite{chodera2016,grossfield2019}.

This problem becomes especially important when estimating uncertainty. Treating dependent frames as independent can make an estimate appear more precise than the available sampling supports.

\section{Well-tempered metadynamics}
Metadynamics encourages exploration by adding a potential along a small set of collective variables (CVs), numerical coordinates chosen to describe relevant changes. The bias depends on where the system has already been. In well-tempered metadynamics, its growth slows as bias accumulates. The bias factor is $\gamma=(T+\Delta T)/T>1$; in the long-time regime the bias approaches a scaled negative free energy, up to a constant \autocite{barducci2008}.

The method changes both forces and sampled frequencies. Statistical reweighting is therefore needed for equilibrium populations. The recorded trajectory time cannot directly supply unbiased relaxation times or transition rates.

\section{Gaussian hills and bias construction}
The accumulated potential can be written as a sum of local Gaussian hills:
\begin{equation}
V(s,t)=\sum_{t_k<t}h_k\exp\!\left[-\frac12\sum_a\frac{(s_a-s_a(t_k))^2}{\sigma_a^2}\right].
\label{eq:hills}
\end{equation}
In \cref{eq:hills}, $s$ is the CV vector, $t_k$ a deposition time, $h_k$ the hill height and $\sigma_a$ its width along coordinate $a$. Well-tempered heights follow $h_k=h_0\exp[-V(s(t_k),t_k)/(k_B\Delta T)]$ in molecular energy units; molar units require $R$ instead of $k_B$.

PLUMED reports the instantaneous bias as \code{metad.bias}. Its offset-corrected output is \code{metad.rbias} = \code{metad.bias} $-$ \code{metad.rct}, where the last term is $c(t)$ \autocite{plumedmetad}. Understanding these distinct quantities is necessary before interpreting an exported bias column.

\section{Free-energy surfaces}
For a CV probability density $p(s)$, free energy is $F(s)=-\beta_T^{-1}\log p(s)+C$. The constant $C$ fixes an arbitrary reference. Integrating over coordinates omitted from the CVs can conceal structural differences: several atomic arrangements can occupy the same location on a two-dimensional surface.

PLUMED's \code{sum\_hills} reconstructs surfaces from hill records and distinguishes free-energy output from negative-bias output \autocite{plumedsum}. Confusing them can apply the well-tempered scaling factor twice. A valid grid and a documented physical interpretation are therefore separate requirements.

\section{Statistical reweighting}
Under a quasi-static bias, multiplying sampled contributions by weights proportional to $\exp(\beta_T V)$ corrects their relative probabilities. Combining this expression with the well-tempered relation gives the final-bias approximation used in the primary study:
\begin{equation}
\ell_i\equiv\log w_i=-\frac{1-1/\gamma}{RT}F(\alpha_i,\beta_i).
\label{eq:finalweights}
\end{equation}
Equation~\ref{eq:finalweights} assumes molar energy units and an interval for which the bias can be treated as sufficiently stable. Adding a constant to $F$ shifts all log weights equally; normalization removes that shift.

Other estimators account explicitly for the changing bias normalization \autocite{bonomi2009,tiwary2015}. With verified PLUMED offset-corrected output, the optional comparison uses $\ell_i=\mathrm{rbias}_i/(RT)$ \autocite{plumedreweight}. This is a separate estimator, not another name for final-bias weighting.

\section{Structural descriptors}
The four baseline descriptors summarize secondary structure, compactness and solvent exposure. The supplied $\na$ and $\nb$ are scaled continuous CVs that approximate residue counts. They are not exact integer assignments from DSSP, which uses geometrical and hydrogen-bond information \autocite{kabsch1983}. Radius of gyration, $R_g$, measures spatial spread, while SASA measures accessible surface area.

These descriptors also have different units and definitions. Radius of gyration is a length, whereas SASA is an area. In a Euclidean distance between raw descriptor rows, changing a length unit would change that coordinate's contribution without changing the molecule. Normalization makes the comparison explicit, but it does not prove that all four descriptors contain equally useful structural information. The choice of representation is therefore part of the method, not just a plotting preference.

Similar $R_g$ and SASA values need not imply identical residue contacts. Their possible redundancy is a question to test, not an automatic consequence of correlation. Apolar/polar SASA and hydrogen-bond descriptors can provide additional characterization after clustering, once their definitions are established.

\section{Density-based conformational clustering}
A density peak is a point surrounded by many neighbours yet separated from points of higher density. The published workflow repeatedly searches for such centres in random subsets and retains clusters satisfying size and silhouette criteria \autocite{cadenelli2026}. The silhouette compares proximity to one's own cluster with proximity to other clusters \autocite{rousseeuw1987}.

The silhouette of a frame compares its average distance to its own cluster, $a$, with the smallest average distance to another cluster, $b$: $(b-a)/\max(a,b)$. A positive value favours its assigned cluster in the chosen geometry; a value near zero indicates a boundary region. A mean threshold filters the local groups, but it need not reject every ambiguous frame. This explains why the final assignment remains a separate operation.

These geometric criteria make clustering inspectable, but do not establish metastable physical states. Consensus groups local clusters into broader families; final assignment and statistical weighting answer the separate questions of which frames belong to each family and how much each contributes.
